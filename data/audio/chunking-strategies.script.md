## Mental Model

The mental model for chunking is the lossy compression step that sets the ceiling on everything downstream. A chunk is the atomic unit your retriever can return. If a fact is split across two chunks or buried with noise in one, no reranker, no bigger model, and no clever prompt can reconstruct it. So chunking is not preprocessing. It is the single highest leverage, hardest to reverse decision in a retrieval augmented generation pipeline. And it is fundamentally a trade off between retrievability and sufficiency. Small focused chunks embed precisely. Large chunks carry enough context to answer. That tension organizes every technique you will encounter here. Fixed size optimizes nothing but simplicity. Structure aware respects document boundaries. Semantic late and contextual chunking spend compute to keep meaning intact. The right point on the curve depends on your corpus and is only knowable by measuring end to end. That is why chunking cannot be evaluated in isolation but through retrieval augmented generation evaluation. And why the failure modes it creates are exactly what advanced retrieval augmented generation loops exist to recover from.

## Why Chunking Matters

Embedding models produce a single vector for an input text. This vector must capture the aboutness of that text for similarity search to work. When a chunk contains a single coherent idea, the resulting embedding is a clear signal. When a chunk mixes unrelated topics, the embedding becomes an average that represents nothing well. Researchers call this phenomenon topic dilution. Simultaneously, chunks must contain enough context for a large language model to generate a useful answer. A chunk that reads The value increased by thirty seven percent compared to quarter three is useless without knowing what the value refers to. Chunking strategy is the bridge between document structure and retrieval effectiveness. Consider the fundamental trade offs. Chunk size presents a core tension. Smaller chunks of one hundred to two hundred tokens give more precise retrieval and less topic dilution, but may lack context. You also store more vectors. Larger chunks of five hundred to one thousand tokens give more context per chunk and fewer vectors, but risk topic dilution and retrieving irrelevant content alongside relevant content. Chunk boundaries present another trade off. Hard boundaries split at exactly n characters. They are fast and deterministic, but may split mid sentence or mid argument. Soft boundaries split at natural breakpoints. They preserve semantic coherence, but produce variable size chunks.

## Fixed-Size Chunking

The simplest approach splits text into chunks of fixed token or character count, optionally with overlap. You pick a chunk size, say five hundred and twelve tokens, and an overlap of fifty tokens. Then you slide a window across the token sequence. At each step you take the next chunk sized segment, decode it, and record it. Then you move the start position forward by the chunk size minus the overlap. This gives you a list of chunks. Fixed size chunking works well when documents lack clear structure. For example, raw transcripts or optical character recognition output. It also works when processing speed matters more than retrieval precision, or when you need a baseline to compare more sophisticated approaches against. But the overlap problem is real. Overlap exists to mitigate information loss at chunk boundaries. If a critical piece of information straddles two chunks, overlap ensures it appears fully in at least one. However, overlap introduces redundancy. Storage overhead: twenty percent overlap means twenty percent more vectors to store and search. Duplicate retrieval: the same passage may appear in multiple retrieved chunks, wasting context window. Embedding ambiguity: near duplicate chunks produce near duplicate embeddings, distorting similarity distributions. The typical recommendation of ten to twenty percent overlap is a heuristic. The optimal overlap depends on the information density and structure of your documents. For structured documents with clear paragraph boundaries, overlap is often unnecessary if you split at paragraph boundaries instead.

## Recursive Character Splitting

LangChain popularized recursive character splitting. It attempts to split text at the most natural boundary available. The algorithm tries a hierarchy of separators, falling through to less desirable ones only when necessary. You start with a list of separators: double newline, newline, period space, space, and empty string. For a given text and chunk size, you find the first separator that appears in the text. You split the text using that separator. Then you accumulate pieces into a current chunk. If adding the next piece would exceed the chunk size, you flush the current chunk and start a new one. If a single piece itself exceeds the chunk size and there are remaining separators in the hierarchy, you recurse on that piece with the next separator. Otherwise you treat the piece as its own chunk. The separator hierarchy reflects natural document structure. Prefer splitting at paragraph boundaries, then line breaks, then sentences, then words, and only as a last resort split mid word. Recursive splitting is a good general purpose approach. It balances simplicity with awareness of text structure. But it operates on surface level formatting cues rather than semantic content. A paragraph break does not guarantee a topic boundary, and a long paragraph may contain multiple distinct topics that should be separated.

## Sentence-Based Chunking

Sentence based chunking treats sentences as atomic units. You group consecutive sentences until you reach a chunk size limit. You need a sentence boundary detector, such as the one from the Spacy library. You split your text into sentences. Then you iterate through them. You maintain a current chunk and a running token count. For each sentence, you check if adding it would exceed your maximum sentence count or maximum token count. If so, you flush the current chunk and start a new one with this sentence. Otherwise you append the sentence. The advantage is that chunks always contain complete sentences, making them more readable and providing more coherent context to the large language model. The quality of sentence boundary detection matters. Spacy's sentencizer handles edge cases like abbreviations, decimal numbers, and URLs better than naive period splitting.

## Document Structure-Aware Splitting

For documents with explicit structure, like HTML, Markdown, PDFs with headings, or legal documents with numbered sections, leveraging that structure produces dramatically better chunks. Consider Markdown aware splitting. You split the text on heading lines using a regular expression. For each heading, you record its level and title. You also maintain a hierarchy of headers. As you encounter a new heading, you flush the previous content along with a breadcrumb metadata string built from the header hierarchy. For example, Machine Learning greater than Training greater than Batch Size. This breadcrumb can be prepended to the chunk text before embedding, giving the embedding model crucial context about where this content lives in the document hierarchy. Structured elements like tables and code blocks require special treatment. Never split a table across chunks. If a table exceeds the chunk size, consider converting it to a textual description or indexing each row as a separate chunk with the column headers prepended. Keep code blocks intact. Split before or after the code block, not within it. Include surrounding explanatory text with the code. Numbered or bulleted lists often represent a cohesive set of related points. Keep lists together when possible, or include the list preamble with each list item chunk.

## Semantic Chunking

Semantic chunking moves beyond structural cues to detect topic boundaries in the text itself. The core idea is to compute embeddings for each sentence, then identify points where the semantic similarity between consecutive sentences drops sharply, indicating a topic shift. You embed all sentences using a sentence transformer model. Then you compute cosine similarity between each pair of consecutive embeddings. You can set a static threshold, say zero point five, or use a dynamic threshold computed as the mean similarity minus one standard deviation. Split points are those indices where the similarity falls below the threshold. You then build chunks from consecutive sentences between split points. Raw sentence to sentence similarity is noisy. A more robust approach uses window based smoothing. Instead of comparing individual sentences, you compare groups of sentences. You compute the average embedding of a left window of sentences and the average embedding of a right window, then measure similarity between those windows. This smooths out local fluctuations. Semantic chunking requires an embedding pass over every sentence in the corpus. For a corpus of one million documents with an average of fifty sentences each, that is fifty million embedding operations just for chunking, before the actual indexing embedding pass. Consider using a lightweight embedding model for boundary detection, caching sentence embeddings for reuse in the indexing pipeline, or applying semantic chunking only to high value documents where quality matters most.

## Chunk Size Optimization

There is no universally optimal chunk size. The right size depends on the embedding model, the nature of queries, and the downstream use case. The most reliable approach is empirical evaluation. You define a set of candidate chunk sizes, like one hundred and twenty eight, two hundred and fifty six, five hundred and twelve, one thousand twenty four, and two thousand forty eight tokens. For each size, you chunk all documents, embed all chunks, build an index, and then evaluate retrieval quality against a set of queries with ground truth. You measure recall at k or normalized discounted cumulative gain. This gives you a curve showing which chunk size performs best on your specific data. Research and practitioner experience suggests some general guidelines. Two hundred and fifty six to five hundred and twelve tokens is a good default for factual question answering and semantic search where queries target specific facts. Five hundred and twelve to one thousand twenty four tokens is better for complex queries requiring reasoning over multiple related facts. One thousand twenty four to two thousand forty eight tokens is appropriate for summarization or when context continuity is critical. Never exceed the embedding model's maximum input length. That is typically five hundred and twelve for older models, eight thousand one hundred and ninety two for modern ones.

## Parent-Child Document Relationships

A powerful pattern that addresses the chunk size dilemma is parent child document relationships. You index small chunks for precise retrieval, but return larger parent chunks for context. You create parent chunks, say two thousand tokens each with no overlap. Within each parent, you create child chunks, say four hundred tokens each with a small overlap. Each child has an identifier linking it to its parent. At retrieval time, you embed and search only the child texts. When you retrieve a child, you look up its parent and pass that parent to the large language model. This decouples the retrieval unit from the context unit. The child chunk acts as a precise retrieval key, while the parent provides the context needed for generation. This pattern is used in frameworks like LlamaIndex's Sentence Window Node Parser. The trade off is that you need to store both child and parent texts, and there is an extra lookup hop during retrieval.

## Late Chunking

Late chunking, introduced by Jina AI in two thousand twenty four, addresses a fundamental limitation of traditional chunking: context loss. When you chunk a document and then embed each chunk independently, each chunk's embedding is computed without knowledge of the surrounding document. Late chunking reverses the order. First you pass the entire document through the transformer encoder, up to the model's context length. You obtain contextualized token embeddings for every token in the document. Then you chunk the token embeddings and pool within each chunk to produce chunk embeddings. The key benefit is that each token embedding already has full document context from the attention mechanism. Consider a document that begins Einstein developed the theory of general relativity in nineteen fifteen and later contains a paragraph starting His later work on unified field theory. With traditional chunking, the later paragraph's embedding has no reference to Einstein. The pronoun His is unresolved. With late chunking, the token embedding for His already incorporates attention to Einstein from the full document context. The limitation is that late chunking requires the full document to fit within the model's context window. For very long documents, a sliding window approach with overlap can partially mitigate this, but the fundamental context window limitation remains.

## Contextual Retrieval

Contextual retrieval, introduced by Anthropic in two thousand twenty four, addresses context loss at the text level rather than the embedding level. You use an inexpensive large language model to explicitly inject context into each chunk before embedding. For each chunk, you provide the large language model with the full document and the chunk text. You ask it to generate a short contextual preamble of two to three sentences that situates the chunk within the overall document. Include key entities, the document section, and any referents that the chunk alone does not resolve. Then you prepend that preamble to the chunk text. The combined text is what gets embedded and stored. Consider a financial report where Chapter three discusses revenue figures. A chunk might read Revenue increased twenty three percent year over year, driven primarily by expansion in the APAC region. In isolation, this chunk lacks critical context: which company, which fiscal year, which product line. With contextual retrieval, the preamble becomes This chunk is from Acme Corp's twenty twenty four Annual Report, Chapter three on Revenue Analysis, discussing fiscal year twenty twenty four results for the enterprise SaaS product line. Then the original text follows. The result is a much richer embedding that matches queries about Acme Corp. Anthropic reported that contextual retrieval reduced retrieval failure rates by forty nine percent when combined with hybrid search. The cost is real. Every chunk requires a large language model call during ingestion. For a corpus of one hundred thousand chunks, that means one hundred thousand API calls. Prompt caching helps significantly. Since the full document is repeated across all chunks from that document, the document portion of the prompt is cached after the first call, reducing cost by eighty to ninety percent for subsequent chunks from the same document. The approach is best understood as a one time ingestion cost that pays dividends across every subsequent query.

## Proposition-Based Chunking

Proposition based chunking rethinks what a chunk should contain. Introduced by Chen and colleagues in twenty twenty three, this approach decomposes text into atomic, self contained propositions. Individual facts that can stand alone without requiring surrounding context. A traditional chunk might read Founded in nineteen seventy six by Steve Jobs, Steve Wozniak, and Ronald Wayne, Apple initially sold hand built computers. The company went public in nineteen eighty, making more millionaires than any IPO in history up to that point. Proposition based chunking decomposes this into separate facts. Apple was founded in nineteen seventy six. Apple was founded by Steve Jobs, Steve Wozniak, and Ronald Wayne. Apple initially sold hand built computers. Apple went public in nineteen eighty. Apple's nineteen eighty IPO created more millionaires than any previous IPO. Each proposition is a complete, self contained fact. Pronouns are resolved. Implicit subjects are made explicit. To implement this, you use a large language model. You provide the text and ask it to decompose into atomic propositions. Each proposition should express exactly one fact, be self contained with no unresolved references, include necessary context like names, dates, and locations, and be understandable without reading the original text. The large language model returns a list of propositions. Proposition based chunking shines for factoid retrieval. Queries that target a specific, discrete fact. When a user asks When did Apple go public, a proposition containing exactly that fact produces a near perfect embedding match. The trade off is granularity. Propositions are typically ten to thirty tokens, far smaller than conventional chunks. This means dramatically more vectors to store and search. It also means each retrieved proposition carries less context for generation. A common mitigation is to pair proposition based retrieval with a parent child architecture. Retrieve by proposition, but pass the original passage to the large language model. The computational cost of decomposition is significant, as it requires a large language model call per passage. For large corpora, consider applying proposition decomposition selectively to high value or fact dense documents, while using conventional chunking for narrative or expository content.

## Multi-Modal Document Processing

Real world retrieval augmented generation systems rarely ingest clean plain text. Production pipelines face PDFs with mixed layouts, scanned documents, embedded tables, charts, and images. How text is extracted from these documents determines the ceiling for every downstream chunking strategy. Several tools have emerged to handle complex document formats. DocTR is an open source optical character recognition library built on deep learning. It combines a text detection model with a text recognition model to extract text from images and scanned PDFs. DocTR preserves spatial layout information, which is critical for reconstructing reading order in multi column documents. Unstructured provides a higher level abstraction. It offers a partition function that automatically detects element types like titles, narrative text, tables, list items, and images. It returns structured elements with metadata. This is particularly valuable because it distinguishes between element types, enabling type aware chunking downstream. LlamaParse takes a model driven approach, using multimodal large language models to parse complex documents. It handles embedded tables, charts, and even handwritten annotations by treating document understanding as a vision language task rather than a pure optical character recognition problem. You integrate document parsing with chunking by first parsing the PDF into elements. For each element, you determine its type. Tables get their own chunk to avoid splitting them. You accumulate text elements until you reach a chunk size limit. For images embedded in documents, the emerging pattern is to generate textual descriptions using a vision language model, then index those descriptions alongside the surrounding text. A chart showing quarterly revenue trends becomes a text chunk describing the data, the trend, and the key takeaways, making it searchable through the same embedding pipeline used for text. The key principle is to convert everything to text before chunking, but preserve the original modality in metadata for potential downstream use by multimodal large language models.

## Chunk Metadata Enrichment

Even with the best chunking strategy, the raw text of a chunk may not align well with how users phrase queries. Chunk metadata enrichment bridges this gap by generating additional indexed representations for each chunk. One technique is hypothetical question generation. For each chunk, you ask a large language model to generate questions that the chunk could answer, phrased as a user would naturally ask them. These generated questions are embedded alongside or instead of the chunk text. When a user's query closely matches one of the hypothetical questions, retrieval accuracy improves substantially. The query and the hypothetical question occupy the same linguistic register, unlike the query and the original passage text which may use entirely different phrasing. Beyond hypothetical questions, you can generate per chunk summaries that compress the chunk into one or two sentences capturing the core claim or information. This is particularly valuable for long chunks where the embedding may suffer from topic dilution. The summary embedding provides a concentrated signal that complements the full text embedding. Keyword extraction identifies domain specific terms, named entities, and technical vocabulary. These keywords improve hybrid search by providing explicit lexical matching targets, and they enable faceted filtering in the vector database. With enriched metadata, a single chunk can be represented by multiple vectors in the index. One for the full text, one for the summary, and one for each hypothetical question. At query time, a match against any of these vectors surfaces the chunk. This multi vector approach, sometimes called multi representation indexing, significantly broadens the retrieval surface without increasing the volume of text sent to the large language model. Only the original chunk text is passed for generation. The storage overhead is proportional to the number of vectors per chunk. For three hypothetical questions plus a summary, that is a five times increase in vector count. Most vector databases handle this efficiently through metadata filtering, and the retrieval quality improvement typically justifies the cost.

## Practical Chunking Pipeline

A production chunking pipeline typically combines multiple strategies. You start by detecting the document type. For Markdown, you use structure aware splitting with a maximum chunk size of one thousand tokens. For code, you split by functions or classes. For transcripts, you use semantic chunking with a threshold of zero point four. Otherwise, you default to recursive splitting with a chunk size of eight hundred tokens. After chunking, you post process. You skip very short chunks that are likely noise, chunks with fewer than twenty words. If a chunk has a breadcrumb from the structure aware splitter, you prepend it as context. You record the chunk text, its metadata, and its token count. This simple pipeline adapts to different document types while maintaining a consistent output format.

## Runtime Internals

The lossy split sets the ceiling model hides the mechanics that decide whether a chunk is retrievable and sufficient. Recursive splitting with a separator hierarchy does not cut at a fixed offset. It tries an ordered separator list, paragraph, sentence, word, and recurses, falling back only when a unit exceeds the size budget. This keeps semantic units intact when possible. The runtime knob is the separator order. A wrong order that splits on whitespace before paragraphs shreds structure. Overlap as boundary insurance uses a sliding window so that a fact straddling a boundary survives in at least one chunk. The runtime cost is duplication. Too much overlap inflates index size and returns near duplicate chunks that waste the context window. Too little reintroduces the boundary loss failure. Overlap is a recall redundancy dial, tuned against measured boundary miss rate. Parent child small to big retrieval is a powerful runtime pattern. You embed small child chunks for precise matching, but return the parent span to the large language model for sufficiency. This decouples the retrieval unit from the context unit, resolving the core trade off at the cost of a parent store and an extra lookup hop. Contextual and late chunking both spend compute at index time to raise retrieval precision. Contextual chunking prepends a large language model generated situating sentence per chunk. Late chunking embeds the whole document first, then pools per chunk so each vector carries global context. Both are explicit cost quality trades, the same shape as model distillation and compression. The recurring theme is that every advanced chunking method spends index time compute to push the retrieval augmented generation quality ceiling higher. It is only worth it if measured retrieval precision actually moves.
